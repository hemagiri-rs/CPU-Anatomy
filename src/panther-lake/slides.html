Inside Panther Lake

Intel's Core Ultra Series 3 launched in January 2026 as the first client processor built on Intel 18A. Here it sits on a laptop mainboard. We'll take it apart, zoom into the silicon, and watch one core run a program out of order.

01 · Cooling

The cooling module

A copper cold plate presses on the dies. Two flattened heat pipes carry heat to a stack of thin fins at the edge of the board. A blower fan pulls air in through its centre and pushes it out through the fins.

Watch: the blower spins as the module lifts off its four screws.

02 · Package

Soldered to the board

Laptop processors aren't socketed: the package is soldered straight onto pads on the board in a ball grid array. Unlike Lunar Lake, Panther Lake keeps memory off the package. Traces run to four LPDDR5X chips, up to LPDDR5X-9600 on the top parts.

Watch: the package lifts to show the solder pads beneath it.

03 · Foveros

Three tiles on one base

The chip is built from separate dies, called tiles, stacked on a base with Intel's Foveros packaging:

  • Compute tile: Intel 18A. Cores, cache, NPU and memory controller.
  • GPU tile: 12 Xe3 cores, made on TSMC N3E. The 4-core version is made on Intel 3.
  • Platform controller tile: TSMC. PCIe, Thunderbolt, USB and Wi-Fi.

Watch: the tiles lift to show the micro-bumps that join them to the base. Dark filler tiles keep the top flat for the cold plate.

04 · Compute tile

A city of blocks

Zoomed in, the compute tile is about 14.3 × 8.0 mm, roughly 115 mm². It holds 16 cores: 4 performance cores, 8 efficient cores and 4 low-power efficient cores, plus caches, the NPU, media and display engines, and the memory interface.

Block positions are simplified from published annotated die shots.

05 · P-cores

Four Cougar Cove P-cores

The performance cores are the big, wide engines for heavy single-threaded work. Cougar Cove builds on Lion Cove with a larger branch predictor, a 50% larger TLB and better memory disambiguation. Intel claims about 10% more single-thread performance than Arrow Lake and Lunar Lake.

Watch: the scheduler hands work to each P-core in turn.

06 · E-cores

Darkmont E-cores and the LP island

Two clusters of four Darkmont E-cores sit between the P-cores, and each cluster shares a 4 MB L2. They add throughput for multi-threaded work at much lower power per core.

Four more Darkmont cores sit on a separate low-power island, so light background tasks can run while the main cluster sleeps.

07 · Memory hierarchy

18 MB of L3 on a ring

The P-cores and E-cores share an 18 MB L3 cache, split into slices at the stops of a ring bus. Behind it, an 8 MB memory-side cache serves every block on the tile, including the LP island and the NPU, so fewer trips go out to DRAM.

Watch: requests ride the ring stop by stop.

08 · AI and media

NPU 5, media and display

The NPU is a dedicated engine for neural networks: arrays of multiply-accumulate units with their own 4.5 MB of cache. It reaches up to 50 TOPS, adds FP8 support, and is about 40% smaller than Lunar Lake's NPU. The media engine encodes and decodes video; the display engine drives the screens.

09 · To DRAM

The memory interface

Along the back edge, a 128-bit interface in four channels drives the LPDDR5X or DDR5 chips: up to LPDDR5X-9600, or DDR5-7200 on some models. Every cache miss that reaches DRAM leaves through these pads.

Watch: the physical interface signalling.

Structure sizes are Lion Cove's published figures; Intel says Cougar Cove extends them.

11 · Front end

Predict, fetch, decode

The branch predictor (12K-entry branch target buffer) guesses where the program goes next. The 64 KB instruction cache delivers the instructions and 8 decoders turn them into micro-ops. Hot loops are replayed from the micro-op cache, which holds 5,250 micro-ops and delivers 12 a cycle, into a 192-entry queue.

Watch: each loop iteration is fetched in one cycle and decoded into micro-ops.

12 · Out of order

Rename and reorder

Up to 8 micro-ops a cycle are renamed onto physical registers and given a place in the reorder buffer (576 entries). They then wait in the schedulers until their inputs are ready.

That's why the independent add r3 races ahead while add r0 waits 9 cycles for the load.

Watch: the ROB slots fill up; tokens are colour-coded by type.

13 · Execution

18 ports

Each cycle the schedulers send ready micro-ops to 18 execution ports: 6 integer ALUs, 4 vector and floating-point units (two for multiply-add, two for add), and 3 load and 3 store address units. The oldest ready micro-ops go first.

Watch: several ports fire in the same cycle.

14 · Memory pipeline

L0, L1, L2

Loads check a 48 KB L0 cache in 4 cycles, then a 192 KB L1 in 9 cycles, then the core's private 3 MB L2, before going out to L3. The DTLB translates addresses with 128 entries.

Watch: the first load of n misses L0 and is found in L1.

15 · Speculation

Mispredict, flush, retire

The predictor assumes the loop continues, so the core runs ahead into iterations that may never happen. When the last jnz resolves as not taken, every younger micro-op is flushed and fetch restarts at the right address. Results only become official at retirement, in program order, up to 8 a cycle.

Watch for the red flush at the end of the loop.

16 · All together

Run it

The program loads n = 10, adds 10 + 9 + … + 1 = 55, counts the iterations and stores the sum. It retires 45 instructions in 31 cycles, about 1.45 per cycle.
